Original abstract

In Pinus pinea, cone to pine nut yield (total pine nut weight expressed as percentage of cone weight), an important crop trait, is decreasing worldwide. This phenomenon is of great concern, since the nuts of this species are highly demanded. Cone weight, seed and pine nut morphometry, and pine nut yield were monitored in a non-native area in Chile for 10 years. For this purpose, 560 cones, and the seeds and pine nuts contained in them, were counted, measured and weighed in a multi-environment study involving seven plantations. Seed and pine nut damage was evaluated. Two contrasting categories of cone weight (heavy/light) were defined. Cone to pine nut yield (PY) and other traits were calculated and compared between categories using a mixed linear model. Regression trees were used to explain PY variability. Cone weight was higher than in the species’ native range (474 g vs 300 g on average). Pine nut number per cone and PY were significantly higher in the heavy cone category than in the light cone category (125 vs 89 units, and 4.05 vs 3.62%, respectively), The percentage of damaged seeds was lower in heavy than in light cones (9.0% vs 15.9%). Thus, PY depended on seed and pine nut morphometry as well as on seed health. Management practices, such as fertilization and irrigation, could be used to boost production of heavy cones and consequently increase PY.

Live rewrite

Pinus pinea, also called stone pine, produces edible pine nuts. An important feature of this crop is its cone-to-pine-nut yield: the total weight of the edible pine nuts inside a cone, expressed as a percentage of the cone’s weight. The researchers call this PY. This yield is decreasing worldwide, which is worrying because pine nuts from this species are in high demand. For 10 years, researchers studied stone pines in Chile, outside the area where this species grows naturally. They tracked cone weight, seed and pine nut measurements—called morphometry—and pine nut yield. Here, “seed” means the pine nut with its shell still on, while “pine nut” means the edible kernel inside. The study covered seven plantations in different growing environments. The researchers counted, measured and weighed 560 cones and the seeds and pine nuts they contained. They also checked seeds and pine nuts for damage. The researchers defined two contrasting cone-weight groups: heavy and light. They calculated PY and other features and compared the groups using a mixed linear model, a statistical method that can account for several sources of differences, such as plantation and year. They also used regression trees, a statistical tool that repeatedly splits observations into groups, to help explain why PY varied. Cones in the Chilean study were heavier on average than cones in the species’ native range—the area where it grows naturally: 474 g compared with 300 g. The section reports that heavy cones had significantly more pine nuts per cone than light cones, 125 compared with 89, and significantly higher PY, 4.05% compared with 3.62%. “Significantly” means that the statistical test found evidence of a difference, rather than a difference readily explained by random variation alone. However, the paper elsewhere identifies 125 and 89 as counts of seeds with shells, not edible pine nuts; see the editorial notes. Heavy cones also had a lower percentage of damaged seeds than light cones: 9.0% compared with 15.9%. The researchers concluded that PY depended on seed and pine nut measurements as well as seed health. They suggest that fertilization, which supplies nutrients, and irrigation, which supplies water, could help trees produce more heavy cones and, as a result, increase PY.

Editorial notes

  • The section labels 125 versus 89 as pine nuts per cone. The Results and Table 4 identify these as rounded counts of seeds with shells (124.8 versus 89.1). The reported counts of healthy edible pine nuts are instead 112.9 versus 76.1 per cone.
  • The section reports average cone weight as 474 g, consistent with rounding Table 4’s 473.6 g to the nearest gram. The Results and Discussion instead report 470 g, apparently using coarser rounding without explaining it.
  • The suggestion that fertilization and irrigation could increase PY is not a treatment result from this study. The study compared cone-weight groups; the Discussion says the observed differences cannot be attributed to irrigation and calls for further studies of management practices.
